Starting from the basics

What the microbiome actually is — and why it belongs in your differential

A bioinformatics background isn't required to use MANTRA's findings — the three principles below are sufficient context, and the foundation everything else on this site builds on.

01

It's a network, not a species list

Trillions of bacteria, viruses, fungi and archaea act as one interconnected network — not a checklist of "good" or "bad" organisms. Most tools that just count species miss this entirely.

02

It's dynamic — a lever, not a fixed trait

Reshaped continuously by diet, drugs and disease. Unlike a patient's genome, it's something you can act on — which is exactly why it matters for treatment decisions, not just diagnosis.

03

Function generalises. Species don't.

The specific bacteria in a patient's gut vary person to person, even with the same disease. Function is often more conserved than species composition — which is why MANTRA is built to score at that functional level directly, alongside the species-level picture rather than in place of it.

The science behind the principle

Function-first isn't a hunch. It's what the field's largest studies found.

Eight published results, from independent groups, that MANTRA's design rests on.

01

Your gut bacteria carry roughly 150 times more genes than you do.

The human gut microbial gene catalogue holds about 3.3 million genes. Much of the biochemistry happening in the gut is encoded there, not in the patient's own genome.

Qin et al., Nature 2010
02

Which species you carry varies from person to person. What the community does stays remarkably stable.

Across hundreds of healthy people, the Human Microbiome Project found that microbial composition varied widely while the metabolic pathways performed stayed stable. This is the observation MANTRA is built around.

Human Microbiome Project Consortium, Nature 2012
03

About half of the bacteria linked to a disease are linked to other diseases too.

Re-analysing 28 case–control studies across 10 diseases, researchers found roughly half of disease-associated genera respond to more than one condition: a shared response to being unwell, not a specific marker. It is one reason species-only signatures often fail to carry over between cohorts.

Duvallet et al., Nature Communications 2017
04

Gut bacteria chemically modify most of the drugs we prescribe.

When 76 human gut bacterial strains were screened against 271 common oral drugs, 176 of those drugs were significantly depleted by at least one strain. Treatment response and toxicity are partly a microbial question.

Zimmermann et al., Nature 2019
05

The same meal can send two people's blood sugar in opposite directions.

In a cohort of roughly 800 people, identical foods produced widely different glucose responses, and a model that included gut microbiome features predicted those responses better than standard carbohydrate counting.

Zeevi et al., Cell 2015
06

Whether a cancer immunotherapy works can depend on what lives in the gut.

Patients who had recently taken antibiotics responded less well to PD-1 blockade, and the gut bacterium Akkermansia muciniphila was enriched in responders. Transferring responder microbiota into mice improved their response.

Routy et al., Science 2018
07

Environment shapes the gut microbiome far more than genetics does.

In over 1,000 people, host genetics explained only a small share of microbiome variation, while diet, medication and lifestyle explained much more. That is why a microbiome-based readout carries information a genome test cannot.

Rothschild et al., Nature 2018
08

In inflammatory bowel disease, the gut's chemistry shifts with disease activity.

Following people over time with metagenomics, metabolomics and more, the second Human Microbiome Project (HMP2) found that functional and metabolic changes track disease activity, beyond what species counts alone show.

Lloyd-Price et al., Nature 2019
